Chapter 5

Vivaan’s Struggles & Silent Fight

Vivaan sat frozen in his apartment, his fingers curled around his phone, scrolling through headline after headline, each one a dagger cutting deeper into his pride.

"Kapoor Fashion’s PR Disaster: The Man Behind the Failure.""Small-Time Photographer Ruins Decades of Legacy.""Vivaan Mehra—The Name That Drowned Kapoor Fashion."

He exhaled sharply, his throat tightening. The world had turned against him overnight, and the worst part? Dilip Kapoor had handed him over to the wolves.

It had happened so fast. A press conference, Dilip standing before flashing cameras, his expression a mix of disappointment and well-practiced authority. This unfortunate situation arose from a misguided campaign idea handled by an external hire. While Kapoor Fashion takes full responsibility, it is clear that certain individuals were not equipped to handle the brand’s image.

And just like that, Vivaan became the scapegoat.

His phone vibrated—a call from an unknown number. He let it ring. Then another. And another. Messages flooded in.

Vivaan, what the hell happened?Dude, your name is everywhere. You need to clear this up.I warned you, man. You were playing in a league too big for you.

He clenched his jaw, tossing his phone onto the couch. What was there to clear up? The world had already made its decision.

His career was in ruins. Doors that had once been open to him had slammed shut overnight. Agencies that had praised his talent now refused to answer his calls. Friends in the industry distanced themselves, afraid of getting caught in the backlash.

And Kapoor Fashion? The place that had once made him feel like he belonged had chewed him up and spit him out without a second thought.

His mind drifted back to that moment in Rhea’s office—when Dilip had humiliated him, and she had stood there, silent.

He had told himself that she was in shock, that she didn’t know what to say. But no matter how many times he replayed it, the truth remained the same: She hadn’t stopped him from walking away.

A bitter chuckle escaped his lips. "Guess I was never meant to stay."

He leaned back on the couch, staring at the ceiling, feeling the weight of it all. The accusations. The loss. The emptiness.

And beneath it all, the one thought that refused to leave him—

Why hasn’t she called?

Vivaan stood outside the Kapoor Fashion boardroom, staring at the heavy wooden doors. He had spent the past hour debating whether to be here at all. A part of him wanted to walk away—leave behind the humiliation, the whispers, the stares.

But something held him back.

Rhea.

The thought hit him unexpectedly. His fingers twitched at his side. He could call her. Ask if this was even worth fighting for. Maybe she’d say something—anything—to convince him he wasn’t wasting his time.

But then his mind replayed the moment she had let him walk away in silence.

His jaw tightened, and he exhaled sharply. No. She made her choice. And now, I have to make mine.

With that, he pushed open the doors.

The boardroom was tense. The long glass table was filled with Kapoor Fashion’s top executives, marketing strategists, and PR managers. Vivaan had been given a chair at the far end, away from the core leadership—a clear sign of where he stood in their eyes.

At the head of the table, Dilip Kapoor sat with his usual imposing presence, his fingers tapping impatiently against the polished surface.

"Let's get this over with," he said, his voice dripping with irritation. "Tell me what this so-called marketing team has come up with to fix the disaster we’re in."

The head of marketing, Mr. Sharma, cleared his throat. "Sir, we’ve been working on a crisis management plan. If we pivot the campaign message and engage influencers to reinforce the brand’s credibility—"

Dilip scoffed. "Engage influencers? That’s your grand solution? Throwing more money at overpaid socialites who barely understand fashion? Brilliant."

An uncomfortable silence spread across the room. Mr. Sharma hesitated but continued, "Sir, we believe it’s about controlling the narrative. If we—"

"You ‘believe’?" Dilip cut him off. "That’s the problem. Kapoor Fashion isn’t a place for ‘belief.’ It’s a place for results. And so far, all I see is a group of people who have failed to deliver."

A few of the executives shifted uncomfortably. Others exchanged glances but remained quiet.

Vivaan, watching this unfold, clenched his fists under the table. He had seen bad leadership before, but this wasn’t just criticism—this was humiliation.

Dilip turned his gaze to Vivaan, his lips curling into a sneer. "And you. The supposed ‘creative genius.’ What do you have to say? Or have you finally realized you’re out of your depth?"

Vivaan met his gaze evenly. "I have a strategy. A way to salvage Kapoor Fashion’s image."

A scoff came from the other end of the table. Anaya, one of the senior PR executives, leaned forward. "Why should we listen to him? He’s the reason we’re in this mess."

"That’s not entirely fair," Rohit, a marketing manager, interjected. "Vivaan’s campaign worked—until it was misinterpreted. The idea wasn’t wrong. The handling was."

"Exactly," Vivaan said, seizing the opportunity. "The backlash isn’t because we showed the reality of Kapoor Fashion. It’s because we lost control of the message. If we shift the focus, bring back authenticity in a way that’s framed as resilience instead of chaos—"

Dilip let out a low chuckle, shaking his head. "And there it is—the arrogance."

Vivaan frowned. "Sir, this isn’t about arrogance. It’s about fixing the damage."

"Fixing the damage?" Dilip repeated, his voice laced with mockery. He turned to the room. "Do you hear this? The very man who caused the damage is now claiming he can fix it. And you fools are actually entertaining this?"

A few murmurs spread through the room—some in agreement, others in unease.

Dilip leaned back, smirking. "Tell me something, Vivaan. When did you become an expert in running a multi-billion-dollar brand? Was it before or after you started playing with a camera?"

Laughter erupted from some corners of the table.

Vivaan felt the heat rise in his chest, but he forced himself to stay calm. "I may not be an expert in running Kapoor Fashion, but I understand storytelling. And right now, the world is telling a story where Kapoor Fashion is the villain. If we—"

"Enough," Dilip snapped, slamming his hand on the table. "You’ve already done enough damage. If you think I will let a failed photographer dictate how we run this company, you are even more foolish than I thought."

Silence. Cold, cutting silence.

Vivaan looked around the room. Some executives avoided his gaze. Others looked like they wanted to speak up but didn’t dare. Rohit’s jaw was clenched. Even Anaya looked slightly uncomfortable.

But none of them spoke.

Dilip adjusted his cufflinks, then addressed the room. "This company will be rebuilt the right way. My way. Not through childish gimmicks and failed experiments. If any of you have a problem with that, you’re free to leave."

No one moved.

Dilip smirked. "I thought so."

He turned back to Vivaan, his expression twisting into something almost amused. "And you—stick to what you were hired for. If you think throwing around half-baked ideas will earn you a place at this table, you’re mistaken. This is business, not some passion project. Know your limits."

Laughter rippled through the room again, sharp and cutting. Vivaan felt every bit of it, but he refused to look away.

Vivaan exhaled slowly, forcing himself to keep his expression neutral. His hands curled into fists under the table, but he didn’t rise. Leaving now would mean defeat, and he wasn’t about to give them that satisfaction.

He had given them a way out, and they had thrown it back in his face.

As he reached the door, he glanced back at the team—at Rohit, at the ones who had wanted to speak but couldn’t.

Maybe Dilip was right. Maybe Kapoor Fashion didn’t need dreamers.

But dreamers were exactly what would save it.

If no one else would fight for it, he would.

As he stepped into the hallway, his mind was already racing. If the company wouldn’t listen to him, he would find the people who would.

It was time to build his own team.

The morning sun poured through the open windows, casting soft golden hues across the whitewashed walls of the old house. Rhea sat on the verandah, cradling a cup of chai between her hands, watching the distant fields sway under the light morning breeze. This house—her mother’s house—had once been filled with warmth, laughter, and the scent of jasmine from the garden. Now, it was just quiet.

She had been here for three months now. Long enough for the villagers to stop looking at her like an outsider. Long enough for her to recognize familiar faces at the market, for the shopkeeper to know she liked her vegetables fresh, for the neighborhood kids to stop staring at her in curiosity.

But had she truly settled? Or had she simply learned to exist in the quiet?

She had thought leaving would bring clarity. That being here, away from Kapoor Fashion, away from the media, would give her time to breathe. But instead, the silence pressed down on her, filling the spaces where meetings, deadlines, and expectations used to be.

She had told herself she wasn’t running away—just taking time. But was that true? Or had she done exactly what her grandfather always expected—crumbled under pressure?

She picked up her phone, her thumb hovering over the screen. Vivaan. His name was still there in her contacts, untouched for three months.

She typed out a message—something simple, something neutral. How are you?

But the cursor blinked back at her, waiting, judging.

What was she even expecting? That he’d respond as if nothing had happened? That three months of silence could be erased with a few words?

She exhaled, her chest tightening. Maybe it was too late. Maybe he had already moved on.

With a quiet sigh, she deleted the message and set the phone aside.

And Vivaan…

Her fingers tightened slightly around the cup. She had told herself it was better this way. That their time working together had been just that—work. But some days, she wondered if she had misread everything. If she had mattered at all.

If I was important to him, wouldn’t he have tried to contact me?Or did he just move on the moment I left?

She sighed, brushing a loose strand of hair from her face. Her reflection in the metal rim of her cup caught her eye. The woman staring back at her looked different.

Her once perfectly styled hair was longer now, wavy and untamed from months without professional care. Sun exposure had deepened the golden undertones of her skin. She was thinner—not in an unhealthy way, but in a way that suggested a lighter existence, stripped of excess.

No makeup. No designer outfits. Just a simple cotton salwar kameez, her sleeves rolled up, a beaded bracelet loose around her wrist. She barely recognized herself, and yet, for the first time in a long while, she felt like she was seeing herself clearly.

The sound of footsteps on the stone pathway broke her thoughts. Vina Ji, her mother’s childhood friend, stepped onto the verandah, setting down a basket filled with fresh fruits.

"You were supposed to come to the market today," Vina Ji said, brushing dust off her hands. "I told you to help me bargain, but you vanished like a ghost."

Rhea smiled slightly, setting her cup down. "I didn’t vanish. I just—woke up late."

Vina Ji gave her a knowing look. "You wake up at dawn every day. Try again."

Rhea exhaled, shaking her head. "I just didn’t feel like going today."

Vina Ji didn’t press, just sat down beside her. "You should’ve come. The mangoes are good this season. The kind your mother used to love."

Something in Rhea’s chest tightened. She stared down at her cup, tracing the rim with her fingertip. "I remember. She used to say the village ones were sweeter than the ones in the city."

"She wasn’t wrong," Vina Ji said, leaning back in her chair. "But you wouldn’t know, would you? You haven’t been eating much lately."

Rhea let out a soft laugh, shaking her head. "Are you going to start keeping track of my meals now?"

"Someone has to," Vina Ji said with mock seriousness. "If I leave you alone, you’ll survive on tea and overthinking."

Rhea didn’t reply. The conversation drifted to small things—the neighbor’s new cow, the carpenter fixing the temple’s roof, the unpredictability of the monsoon season. Neither of them mentioned Kapoor Fashion. Neither of them mentioned Mumbai.

But they both knew the silence wasn’t empty

Kapoor Fashion was sinking. And this time, there was no one to stop it.

The stock market told the story before the newspapers did. Share values had dropped another six percent overnight, wiping out crores in valuation. The headlines were brutal.

"Kapoor Fashion’s Golden Era is over."

"Investors Flee as Brand Faces Its Worst Quarter in Years."

"Dilip Kapoor’s Leadership under Fire – Is the Empire Crumbling?"

Board members scrambled to reassure investors, but the damage was done. Big-name retailers began reconsidering contracts, distributors hesitated to restock products, and competitors wasted no time launching aggressive marketing campaigns to steal Kapoor Fashion’s market share.

In a quiet corner of the office, away from the collapsing empire, Vivaan sat in front of his laptop, eyes scanning data, reading trends, making notes.

He wasn’t done yet.

But no one needed to know that. Not yet.

Meanwhile, at Kapoor Fashion headquarters, Dilip Kapoor paced in the executive boardroom, his usual air of control cracking under pressure. Across the table, the financial team sat stiffly, pages of red-marked reports scattered in front of them.

"How bad is it?" Dilip asked, his voice gruff.

The CFO hesitated, clearing his throat. "We’re looking at a projected 30% revenue loss this quarter. Major investors are asking for reassurances—or their money back."

"That’s not an option," Dilip snapped, fingers pressing into his temples. "We need a strategy. A real one."

One of the senior finance heads, Mr. Verma, shifted uncomfortably. "Sir, without a concrete turnaround plan, we may have to consider—"

"We are not considering anything drastic," Dilip cut in, his tone sharp. "This is a temporary setback. We just need to manage the numbers until the media finds something else to focus on."

"And how exactly do we ‘manage’ a loss this big?" another executive dared to ask.

Dilip exhaled sharply, his patience thinning. "We shift budgets. Delay payments. Push supplier negotiations. Find a way to keep this quiet."

The room fell into an uneasy silence.

They all knew what he meant. Cover it up. Stall until they could figure out a way to clean up the mess.

But Kapoor Fashion was bleeding out faster than anyone was willing to admit. And for the first time in decades, Dilip Kapoor didn’t have a real solution.

Vivaan sat across from his parents, fingers tapping restlessly against the table. His mother, Neelam Mehra, poured chai into his cup, her warm smile not quite reaching her eyes. His father, Raj Mehra, sat with his arms crossed, his expression unreadable.

"Vivaan, beta," his mother started, her voice soft but firm. "You’ve been putting this off for too long. It’s time to move forward with Sanya."

"Maa, I’ve been busy," Vivaan replied, forcing a polite tone. "Work has been hectic."

"Work is always hectic," Raj interjected. "That doesn’t mean life has to stop. Sanya is a good girl, from a respectable family. The engagement is already delayed enough."

Vivaan exhaled, staring at his untouched chai. Was this really happening? Was he about to let his life be decided for him just because he didn’t have the strength to argue?

"I just… I don’t know if I’m ready yet," he admitted.

His mother reached across the table, squeezing his hand. "You’re 28, Vivaan. You have responsibilities—not just to yourself, but to us, to Sanya, to both families."

His chest tightened. Responsibilities. Expectations. The weight of everyone else’s wishes pressing down on him.

Just before the engagement day Vivaan’s mind was racing. Was he really ready to marry? The thought echoed in his head like a cruel reminder of everything he was avoiding. He had spent years building his life around what his parents expected, what the world wanted from him. But what about what he wanted?

He tried to push it away, focusing on the conversation, on his family, but the question lingered. Do I really need to marry her? Is this what I want? His eyes flickered to the engagement ring on his finger. He wasn’t even sure it was the right choice.

He had spent his life making choices to please others. And now, standing on the edge of this major decision, it felt like he was losing control of his own life.

What happens if I don’t follow through? A sharp pain hit his chest. I can’t break my family’s trust.

His thoughts were interrupted as his father spoke, reminding him of his duties, his obligations. The weight of his family's expectations pressed down even harder.

He couldn’t back out now. Could he?

The engagement ceremony was grand, dazzling, suffocating. Vivaan stood beside Sanya, cameras flashing, relatives congratulating them. Sanya was beautiful, elegant in her shimmering saree, smiling at him like she had no doubts in the world.

He tried to smile back. But inside, he felt nothing.

The cheers, the laughter, the endless talking—all of it felt like white noise. Was this what his life was supposed to be?

A voice in his head whispered, If not this, then what?

He glanced at his hands, at the engagement ring now sitting heavy on his finger. This wasn’t what he wanted.

Later that night, Vivaan sat alone in his dark room, staring at the camera that had once been his greatest passion. It sat untouched on his desk, collecting dust.

His entire life had been about choices—the ones he had made, the ones that had been made for him. And now, here he was, standing at the edge of a decision that didn’t feel like his own.

He thought of his parents. Their pride, their expectations. The way his mother had held his hand, asking him to think about the family’s reputation. The way his father had spoken about duty, responsibility.

I can’t break their trust.

The weight of it all settled on his chest, heavy, suffocating. He had spent his whole life trying to live up to their image of him. Trying to be the son they could proudly talk about.

But what about what he wanted?

What did he even want anymore?

His gaze flickered back to the camera. Photography had been his escape, his passion, his purpose. But now? Now, it felt like a distant memory. The thought of picking it up again felt empty, meaningless.

Who was he, if not the man behind the lens?

His phone buzzed on the table, shaking him from his thoughts.

A news update.

He glanced at the screen, his heart skipping a beat.

Kapoor Fashion Faces Financial Collapse – Investors Threaten to Withdraw Support

The breath caught in his throat. The company was falling apart.

His fingers hovered over his messages, over a familiar name he hadn’t typed in months.

Rhea.

His breath hitched. His chest felt heavy.

She was the only one who could fix this.

Rhea walked down the narrow path in her mother's village, the air crisp with the scent of fresh earth. It had been months since she had arrived, months since she had retreated into the quiet rhythms of this place. The once constant hum of Kapoor Fashion felt like a distant echo, swallowed by the peace of the village life.

She looked different now. Her hair, once tangled with stress and long nights, was neatly pulled back, a few strands gently framing her face. Her posture was more confident, less burdened by the weight she had carried. There was a calmness about her—no longer the girl drowning in doubt, but someone who had started to find herself again. The old vulnerability that had plagued her was now a quiet strength. The intense gaze she had once reserved for her father’s company was now softer, focused on something deeper—herself.

She found Vina Chachi in the small garden behind the house, carefully tending to a row of marigolds. Rhea smiled softly, offering her assistance.

“Chachi, need help with the flowers?” she asked, kneeling beside her.

Vina Chachi smiled warmly but didn’t speak. They worked in silence for a few minutes, the rhythmic pull of weeds and the gentle patting of soil filling the space between them. It was a simple, peaceful moment, but Rhea couldn’t escape the heavy thoughts that always seemed to follow her.

“So,” Vina Chachi broke the silence, her voice casual but knowing, “how’s the work going?”

Rhea chuckled lightly, shaking her head. “You know I’m not working anymore, Chachi. Just helping out here.”

“Yes, I know,” Vina Chachi replied, her tone light but piercing. “But how are you really doing? I can see the quiet in your eyes, Rhea. But I don’t see peace.”

Rhea paused, fingers stilling on the marigold petals. "I’ve learned to accept that things are... complicated. I don’t think I’m ready to face Kapoor Fashion again.”

Vina Chachi studied her for a moment, then smiled as if she had heard this before. “Well, just because you’re not facing it doesn’t mean the world stops turning.”

Rhea was about to respond, but a voice from the television inside the house caught her attention. The sound of an anchor speaking was unmistakable, and as the words echoed through the open window, Rhea’s heart sank.

The TV displayed a bold headline:

"Kapoor Fashion Faces Massive Losses: Investors Pulling Out After a Disastrous Quarter"

The report continued, showing clips of Dilip Kapoor making statements about the company’s declining fortunes and failed strategies, his face a mask of pride and frustration. The news continued, flashing figures that showed the company’s market value crashing, while Rhea stood frozen in place.

Dilip had tried, as always, to control the narrative, but the damage was done. The company was falling apart without her—and she knew it. The weight of her father’s legacy, the future of Kapoor Fashion, it all sat in the pit of her stomach.

Vina Chachi walked into the room, carrying two cups of tea, as Rhea stared blankly at the screen. The news continued its unflinching attack on the company.

Vina Chachi placed a cup of tea gently in front of Rhea. “You’ve seen this, haven’t you?”

Rhea nodded but said nothing. Her mind was reeling. She thought back to the days when her father had entrusted her with everything—the company, his legacy. She had promised him that she would make it work. And now... now, it was unraveling.

Vina Chachi sat beside her, her presence a quiet anchor. “Rhea, I know you’re tired. I know you’re hurt. But you can’t hide forever.”

“I can’t go back,” Rhea whispered, her voice trembling. “I’ve failed. I’ve let them down. I don’t have the strength to prove to everyone that I can do it. I don’t even know if I believe in myself anymore.”

Vina Chachi sighed softly, taking Rhea’s hands in hers. “You’re not going back to prove anything to anyone, Rhea. Not to your grandfather, not to the world, not even to your father. You’re going back for yourself.”

Rhea looked up, her eyes brimming with uncertainty. “For myself?”

“Yes, Rhea. Your father’s dream wasn’t just about the company. It was about you. He believed in you not because you had to be perfect, but because you had the heart to keep going when things got tough. You owe it to yourself—not to anyone else—to finish what you started.”

Rhea’s breath hitched in her throat. For herself. She had spent months running away from the pressure, from the weight of her responsibilities. But in that moment, she realized the truth. She wasn’t running from the company—she was running from herself.

A spark, faint at first, began to ignite deep within her chest. This company wasn’t just her responsibility—it was her father’s legacy. She could feel his presence around her, his belief in her. She wasn’t the girl who had failed. She was the girl who had yet to finish the fight.

Vina Chachi squeezed her hands gently. “You’ve already proven to everyone else what they wanted. Now, prove it to yourself. You don’t need to answer to anyone else. Only you.”

Tears welled up in Rhea’s eyes as she nodded. A wave of emotion crashed over her, but it wasn’t sadness—it was resolved. Her father’s legacy wasn’t just a title. It was the fight she needed to win, not for anyone else but for herself.

She stood up suddenly, her heart beating faster now, as if the weight had lifted, replaced with a new purpose.

“I’m going back,” she said, her voice clear and strong. “I’m going to fight for Kapoor Fashion.”

Vina Chachi smiled, a proud look in her eyes. “That’s my girl.”

Rhea walked to the window, the same window where she had stared out into the world months ago, feeling lost. Now, the view felt different. It felt like the beginning of something new, a spark that could light the way.

She wasn’t running anymore. She was fighting.

And she was going to win.
