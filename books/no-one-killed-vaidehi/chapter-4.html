Chapter 4

Brakes Failed

The morning light filtered through the trees as Veer stepped onto the farmhouse grounds, the gravel crunching beneath his shoes. A familiar silence hung in the air, the kind that always lingered at crime scenes long after the chaos was over. Beside him, the junior cop matched his pace. They were back to look again, just in case something had been missed.

Veer (looking around absently, voice low):

“Did you try tracing the number that called Raghu?”

Junior (with pride, almost smug):

“No, sir. I figured it’d be a burner phone. I mean, who uses a traceable number to hire a hitman?”

Veer (stops walking, turns slowly to him):

“You figured?”

Junior (a little unsure):

“I mean... yeah. It’s obvious, right?”

Veer (dryly):

"Obvious is the favorite word of lazy detectives."

Junior gulps. Veer steps closer, calm but firm.

Veer:

"Listen to me. Never assume. Especially not in murder cases. Our job isn’t to guess what the criminal probably did—it’s to make sure what he did doesn't go unseen. Even if it’s 99% likely he used a burner... what if he didn’t?"

Junior (quietly):

“But the odds…”

Veer:

“Odds don’t matter when someone’s already dead.”

He crouches near the ashes, brushes something aside with a stick.

Veer:

"Your instinct will grow with time, but discipline? You need that from day one. You check everything. Always. Even if it’s boring. Even if you’re sure it’s nothing."

Junior stands quietly. Veer picks up a burnt twig, tosses it aside.

Veer (without looking up):

“You know why there’s no such thing as a perfect crime?”

Junior (shaking head):

“No, sir.”

Veer:

“Because criminals don’t know when to stop. There’s always one mistake. One ego-driven slip. One blind spot. And we’re not smart enough to predict it. We just have to be thorough enough to catch it.”

He stands, wipes his hands.

Veer:

"Next time you say ‘obvious’ to me, it better be followed by ‘but I still checked.’ Got it?"

Junior (nodding fast):

“Yes, sir. Got it.”

Junior (after a pause, still processing):

“Sir… if we’ve already checked everything here, why are we back?”

Veer (half-sighs, half-grumbles):

“Mehar said something might’ve been missed.”

Junior (curious):

“Really? I didn’t think you actually listened to her theories.”

Veer (glancing sideways):

“And why’s that?”

Junior:

“I mean, you always joke around with her. Ignore her suggestions. I just assumed you didn’t think she was… you know… capable.”

Veer (stops walking again, looks straight ahead):

“You assumed wrong.”

Junior (taken aback):

“Oh. I just thought—”

Veer (cuts in, softer now):

“We joke around because if we don’t, this job will eat us alive. We see burnt bodies, crying parents, psychopaths in clean shirts… you either learn to laugh or learn to drink.”

Junior nods, quietly. Veer continues, his voice lower, almost lost in thought.

Veer:

“She’s good. Better than good. Has instincts most rookies take ten years to develop. She knows I trust her. I don’t need to spell it out.”

Junior:

“But still, you never—”

Veer (dryly):

“Because I’m not a greeting card, Sharma. I don’t walk around telling people they matter.”

Junior (grinning):

“She must be special then. I’ve never seen you backtrack just because someone suggested a revisit.”

Veer (smirks, mutters while walking away):

“Yeah, she’s annoying like that. Makes me double-check my own gut.”

The two walk off toward the trees, faint morning sunlight filtering through. In the silence that follows, it’s clear: Veer trusts Mehar more than he lets on—and maybe, even more than he admits to himself.

They had combed through every corner of the farmhouse.

The sun was now slightly harsher, casting long shadows behind the trees. The silence was thick, except for the occasional chirp of a bird or the rustle of dry leaves. Veer and the junior cop stood facing the last camera, its wires frayed, and its body was in two pieces.

The junior dusted his hands and straightened up.

Junior:

“Sir, I’ve checked every camera. Nothing’s there. And obviously, there was no camera in the bathroom — where Vaidehi was supposedly hit.”

Veer didn’t respond.

The junior glanced at the broken camera beside them.

Junior:

“This one’s dead too. Might’ve shown who dragged her out to the forest, but… Manav broke it, right?”

“Even if it had survived the night, it’s too dark to identify anyone in footage like that.”

Veer finally spoke, his eyes still fixed on the camera.

Veer:

“We don’t even know for sure if Vaidehi is actually dead.”

The junior turned, frowning.

Junior:

“But the body—”

“Burnt beyond recognition,” Veer cut in. “DNA's pending. Until then, nothing’s certain.”

He bent down and picked up the broken camera, handing it to the junior.

Veer:

“Take it. Try to recover as much footage as you can.”

Junior:

“But sir, even if we get something—it’s night footage. Recognizing the killer in that kind of lighting is—”

“The camera didn’t record the murder,” Veer interrupted again, voice flat.

The junior blinked.

Junior:

“What?”

“Paras claimed Manav smashed the camera that night,” Veer explained. “And Vaidehi was still alive at that point. If Paras is telling the truth, the camera never got the chance to capture what happened next.”

The junior was quiet for a second.

Junior:

“Then why recover it?”

Veer:

“Because maybe, just maybe, we missed something else. Don’t ask. Just do it.”

The two walked back in silence, dust clinging to their boots, the broken camera now tucked under the junior's arm like a fragile clue from a ghost that never quite showed up.

In the office, Paras enters with his usual swagger, wearing sunglasses indoors for no reason. Abhay is sitting with legs up on a chair, watching anime on his phone with full focus.

PARAS

(raises an eyebrow)

Abhay, why are you watching a cartoon in the office? That too with surround sound?

ABHAY

(not looking up)

It's not a cartoon. It's ANIME, you idiot.

PARAS

(grinning)

Ohhh right. Anime. The fancy word for emotionally constipated people punching each other in slow motion for 20 episodes.

ABHAY

(shouts without looking)

IT’S ANIME!!!

PARAS

(leans against the chair, unfazed)

Haan haan. And I’m the Hokage of Borivali East.

(deep breath)

Anyway, I think you should stop watching this and hit the gym. Make two more packs to that six-pack body of yours. Then come back and scare people with abs, not anime.

ABHAY

(still annoyed)

Whatever. You old people have no taste.

PARAS

Old?! Kid, I’m the classic version. Like Shaktimaan reruns. Timeless.

ABHAY

(sighs)

What are you doing here anyway?

PARAS

Meeting. Manav called. Said, "Come to the meeting room." I asked what for, and he started explaining… so I cut the call.

My guess? It’s a meeting to decide the topic for our next meeting.

ABHAY

(confused)

Why do you old people have so many meetings? I call Kritika, she says, "I'm in a meeting." Every damn time.

PARAS

(grinning)

Yeah bro, that’s our secret hobby. We sit around and debate whether to order burgers or sandwiches for lunch. Adds meaning to our lives.

(The door swings open. Kritika enters — her expression stormy, her walk loud. Her heels echo with intent.)

PARAS

(under his breath)

Speaking of meaning, the Queen of Thunder has arrived.

KRITIKA

(to Abhay)

What are you doing here?

PARAS

(jumps in before Abhay)

This little kid is watching cartoons because his sugar mommy dumped him here.

ABHAY

(yells, exasperated)

IT’S ANIMEEEE!!

How many times do I have to tell you people!?

KRITIKA

(snarling)

Shut up, Abhay! You should be at the gym. Your competition’s next week.

ABHAY

(defensive)

Didn’t you request me to drop you here?

(throws his arms)

Saying I've had enough gym already!

PARAS

(jumps in, mock dramatic)

Oho! My sexy, super-hot queen requested you? Wow, Abhay bhai. Big day!

ABHAY

(sternly)

I took it as a request. That’s all.

KRITIKA

(boiling)

GO. TO. GYM. NOW.

Abhay scrambles to pick up his stuff and rushes out without another word.

PARAS

(laughing, shakes his head)

Yep. Totally a request.

(smirking)

Anyway, how’s my future wife doing?

Kritika ignores him. Just then, Satyam and Manav walk in. Satyam walks in mid-rant.

SATYAM

(over Paras)

How is she your future wife, Paras?

Do you even care about anything?

Arnav died yesterday, and here you are, joking around like this is a stand-up show.

The room tenses up slightly. Paras looks at him, unfazed.

PARAS

(pointing at them)

Why are you entering with Manav, haan?

MANAV

(casually)

His car's with the mechanic. So I picked him up.

SATYAM

(to Manav, fuming)

And you still didn’t answer my question —

Why did you let the driver go home and made me drive?

MANAV

(shrugging)

Driver’s face wasn’t visible from my seat.

Paras bursts out laughing, trying to hold it in but failing.

PARAS

(tearing up)

Brooo... I swear—

This is why I love this job. Premium entertainment. You all should charge tickets.

SATYAM

(snarling at Paras)

Your job is to handle PR, not run a comedy circus.

KRITIKA

(cutting in, tense)

Forget PR. Veer, that cop, is suspecting us.

After the murder… it’s obvious we’re being watched.

MANAV

(irritated, commanding tone)

When was I supposed to know that Ishani and Vaidehi were sisters?

And how the hell did Vaidehi even get hired here?

Aren’t we supposed to run background checks?

KRITIKA

(defensive)

They weren’t real sisters, okay?

They were friends from an orphanage.

There was nothing on paper connecting them.

And Ishani’s name… it never even hit the news.

Nobody interviewed her. No obituaries. I’ve been trying to dig something up but… nothing yet.

A silence. Satyam’s voice drops a little, a rare vulnerability showing through.

SATYAM

(quietly)

If Ishani had just… cooperated, she might’ve still been alive.

(pauses)

I really miss her.

PARAS

(nods)

Yeah… me too. She was the key player of our team.

And a masterclass in pretending she liked Satyam.

Satyam shoots him a cold look. Paras grins, unfazed.

KRITIKA

(snapping)

She’s the one who did suicide, remember?

If she couldn’t face reality, how is that our fault?

This isn’t the topic of discussion!

(beat)

Arnav is dead. That’s what we need to focus on.

PARAS

(leans back, smugly)

If you weren’t busy doing yo-yo with Abhay instead of me,

You’d know his servant was the one behind it.

MANAV

(sharp)

Kritika’s right. We don’t know who did it yet.

KRITIKA

(sighs, professional)

Raghu has no criminal history.

He was with Arnav for ten years.

The only person Arnav actually trusted with his house.

(pauses)

All I’ve found so far is—Raghu got a suspicious message.

But the number’s a dead end. I’ll find it, but… it’ll take time.

Manav paces once, then stops and points one by one.

MANAV

Kritika — you find who was behind that number.

(turns to Satyam)

Satyam — dig into how Vaidehi got into our company.

How she slipped through our background checks.

(looks at Paras)

And Paras — make Veer and Mehar our clients.

Do your damn PR magic and flip them.

SATYAM

(nods)

I’ll check things here today, then head to our base tomorrow.

Her real records are probably there.

KRITIKA

(suspicious)

Why not today?

SATYAM

(half-smiling)

Gotta pick up my car.

Paras smirks. Kritika sighs. Manav runs a frustrated hand through his hair.

They all fall silent, the gravity of their situation settling in as the scene fades.

The street is dimly lit. Neon glows reflect off parked scooters. Satyam walks toward the garage, phone in one hand, annoyance in his stride.

SATYAM

(sarcastic, dry)

Let me guess — it’s “as good as new”?

MECHANIC

(grinning)

Better. Like the showroom condition, sir.

SATYAM

(rolls eyes)

I know your "showroom" repairs. Anyway... here.

(hands over a card)

The mechanic takes the debit card, swipes it on a dusty POS machine, then hands Satyam the bill along with the card.

SATYAM

(glancing at the bill)

The invoice says yesterday’s date. Why?

MECHANIC

(shrugs, casual)

Tax filing, sir. Small adjustment. Hope that’s okay.

Satyam gives him a half-hearted glare, folds the bill, and tosses it into the glovebox.

SATYAM

You fix cars. Not tax systems. Try sticking to one.

He shuts the glovebox, starts the engine, and drives off into the night.

Satyam’s car glides through the calm, mostly empty roads. Radio on low. He looks tired. The city lights flicker outside.

Halfway home, on a slight downward slope, he taps the brake.

SATYAM

(muttering)

What the hell...

He presses harder. The brake pedal sinks uselessly. No response. The car speeds up.

SATYAM

(panic rising)

Oh shit. SHIT!

He yanks the handbrake — it jerks but doesn't slow the car enough. Horns blare. A truck appears up ahead, trying to turn across the road. There's no time.

Satyam swerves, but not enough — the car SLAMS into the side of the truck at full speed. Glass shatters. The windshield explodes inward. Shards pierce his waist and neck. His body slumps, instantly lifeless, blood seeping onto the seat. The night returns to silence — only the faint sound of the radio static and distant sirens remains.
