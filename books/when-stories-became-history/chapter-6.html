When Stories Became History

Chapter 6

The Mahabharata: A Story Within a Story

If the Ramayana tells us the many lives of Rama's story, the Mahabharata takes us into an entirely different kind of narrative world.

At its centre is a family conflict: two branches of the Kuru dynasty, the Pandavas and the Kauravas, whose rivalry eventually leads to the great war of Kurukshetra.

But the Mahabharata is much more than the story of that conflict.

It tells us about generations of kings, marriages, rivalries, friendships, promises, curses, kingdoms and extraordinary individuals. It contains philosophical discussions, moral dilemmas, stories of other heroes and accounts of events that took place long before the birth of its central characters.

A story about a family dispute becomes a narrative about an entire world.

And perhaps the most interesting feature of the Mahabharata is the way it presents these stories. Many narratives exist inside the larger account, told by one character to another, often becoming important in their own right.

It is a story that constantly opens the door to another story.

Vyasa and the Traditional Account

Tradition identifies the sage Vyasa, also known as Krishna Dvaipayana Vyasa, as the composer of the Mahabharata.

He occupies a particularly important position in the epic because he is not only regarded as its author but also appears as a character within its narrative.

According to the traditional account, Vyasa is the father of Dhritarashtra, Pandu and Vidura through the practice of niyoga. This makes him directly connected to the royal family whose conflict forms the central narrative.

The traditional story also associates Vyasa with the composition of the epic and Ganesha with its writing. According to this account, Ganesha agrees to write down the composition on the condition that Vyasa recites it without interruption, while Vyasa sets his own condition that Ganesha must understand every verse before writing it.

This familiar account gives the Mahabharata a fascinating identity: its traditional author is also one of the figures who helps shape the events described within it.

The epic itself presents a layered account of how its story is transmitted.

The narrative of the Mahabharata is traditionally associated with a great gathering of sages at Naimisharanya. There, the storyteller Ugrasrava Sauti recounts what he has heard about the epic.

The story he tells includes an earlier narration by Vaishampayana, who recites the Mahabharata before King Janamejaya during his serpent sacrifice. Vaishampayana, in turn, presents the account associated with Vyasa.

This creates several levels of narration:

- Vyasa is traditionally identified as the composer.

- Vaishampayana recites the account before Janamejaya.

- Ugrasrava Sauti retells the narrative to the assembled sages.

The story is therefore presented through a chain of narrators, each bringing the larger account to another audience.

The traditional attribution to Vyasa gives the epic its recognised authorial identity, while this layered structure places storytelling and recitation at the very heart of the composition.

The Expanding Narrative of the Mahabharata

The Mahabharata is traditionally divided into eighteen major books, known as Parvas. Together, they cover an enormous range of events, beginning with the ancestry of the Kuru dynasty and extending far beyond the war itself.

The central narrative follows the lives of the Pandavas and Kauravas, from their family relationships and childhood rivalries to the events that lead to their eventual conflict.

But the epic does not move directly from one major event to another.

It frequently pauses to explain earlier events, introduce other characters or explore questions raised by the main narrative. A conversation can lead to a story about an ancient king. A question about duty can lead to a philosophical discussion. A reference to someone's ancestry can introduce an entire genealogy.

These additions give the epic extraordinary depth.

The Mahabharata also preserves a traditional account of its own expansion. In its introductory material, it refers to different forms of the composition, including Jaya, Bharata and the larger Mahabharata.

These are traditionally associated with compositions of different lengths, often described as approximately 8,800, 24,000 and 100,000 verses respectively, with variations in how the figures are presented.

This account is particularly interesting because it reflects an understanding of the epic as something that grew in scale.

The Mahabharata is also enormous in its surviving form. Its Sanskrit text contains approximately 100,000 verses, depending on the recension and method of counting, making it one of the longest epic compositions in world literature.

Its scale is not simply the result of a lengthy account of a war. Much of its size comes from the enormous amount of material surrounding that central conflict.

The literary history of the Mahabharata is equally complex. Scholars studying its language, structure and manuscript traditions have identified different layers of composition and transmission. The epic developed over a long period, rather than appearing in the complete form familiar to modern readers at a single moment.

John Brockington's The Sanskrit Epics (1998) examines this development in detail, including the relationship between the epic's traditional identity and its literary history.

The traditional story of expansion and the scholarly study of textual development are different kinds of accounts, but both draw attention to the remarkable scale and complexity of the Mahabharata.

Embedded Stories, Genealogies and Legends

One of the most distinctive features of the Mahabharata is its collection of stories within stories.

Some of these narratives are relatively independent of the central conflict. Others provide parallels to the experiences of the main characters. Some explain an important idea, while others explore a moral question through the lives of different individuals.

Consider the story of Nala and Damayanti.

This narrative appears in the Vana Parva, during the Pandavas' exile. Yudhishthira is distressed by the loss of his kingdom and the hardships faced by his family. In response, the sage Brihadashva tells him the story of King Nala, who also loses his kingdom through gambling and experiences separation from his wife, Damayanti.

The story provides more than entertainment. It reflects Yudhishthira's own circumstances and offers a parallel through which he can understand his suffering.

The Mahabharata does not simply tell the audience what happened to the Pandavas. It introduces another story that allows the audience to explore similar experiences through different characters.

The story of Savitri and Satyavan offers another example.

Savitri chooses Satyavan as her husband despite knowing that he is destined to die within a year. When Yama arrives to take his life, Savitri follows him and uses her intelligence, determination and devotion to secure her husband's return.

This story, also found in the Vana Parva, has become one of the most widely remembered narratives associated with the Mahabharata.

Then there is the Ramopakhyana, a narration of Rama's story that appears within the same book.

During the Pandavas' exile, Yudhishthira hears the account of Rama from the sage Markandeya. The Ramayana therefore appears as a story within the Mahabharata, connecting two of India's greatest epic traditions.

These examples reveal how the Mahabharata brings together different narrative traditions.

Genealogies and Earlier Generations

The epic also spends considerable time explaining the ancestry of its characters.

The Kuru dynasty does not begin with the Pandavas and Kauravas. Its history stretches across generations of kings and families, connecting the central conflict with a much larger account of royal lineages.

The stories of Shantanu, Ganga, Satyavati, Bhishma and the earlier generations of the Kuru family establish the circumstances that eventually lead to the birth of the central characters.

Other genealogies connect the narrative with different royal dynasties, sages and legendary figures.

These accounts serve several purposes. They establish relationships, explain inheritance, provide historical and legendary background, and connect different narratives within a common framework.

They also create opportunities for additional storytelling.

An ancestor mentioned briefly can become the subject of another episode. A marriage can introduce a new family. A royal lineage can connect one story with another.

The Mahabharata's world is therefore not limited to the people involved in the Kurukshetra war. It extends backwards and sideways through an enormous network of relationships and narratives.

The Harivamsha and Related Narratives

The Mahabharata's narrative world also extends beyond its eighteen major books.

One important example is the Harivamsha, traditionally regarded as a supplementary work associated with the epic.

The name Harivamsha refers to the lineage of Hari, an important name associated with Vishnu. Its contents include genealogical accounts and narratives concerning Krishna, particularly his ancestry, birth and early life.

While Krishna already occupies a central position in the Mahabharata, the Harivamsha provides a much more extensive account of the traditions surrounding him.

It connects the story of Krishna with the broader genealogical and mythological world of the epic.

The Harivamsha is traditionally known as the 'Khila', or supplement to the Mahabharata. Its relationship with the main epic is significant because it demonstrates how related narrative material could develop beyond the boundaries of the central composition.

The stories associated with Krishna did not remain limited to his role as the guide and ally of the Pandavas. They developed into a much larger narrative tradition, exploring his childhood, family, relationships and extraordinary deeds.

Over time, Krishna became the central figure of numerous independent literary and devotional traditions.

Other works and narrative traditions also developed around characters and episodes connected with the Mahabharata. Stories that were once part of a larger epic environment could become the subjects of separate compositions, performances and retellings.

The boundary between a main narrative and its surrounding stories was therefore not always the end of storytelling.

Sometimes, it was the beginning of another tradition.

The Many Layers of the Epic

The Mahabharata contains several kinds of material, and they do not all serve the same purpose.

There is the central account of the Kuru family and its conflict. There are genealogies that establish the background of its characters. There are legends about earlier generations, stories of other kings and heroes, philosophical discussions, moral teachings and narratives that explore different aspects of human behaviour.

The Bhagavad Gita, for example, appears within the Bhishma Parva, as Krishna instructs Arjuna on the battlefield before the war begins.

Its discussion of duty, action, knowledge, devotion and spiritual understanding has become one of the most influential philosophical texts in Indian traditions.

The Shanti Parva and Anushasana Parva, which follow the war, contain extensive discussions on kingship, duty, morality, governance and conduct.

These sections are very different in character from the dramatic events of the battlefield, yet they are part of the same epic.

This diversity gives the Mahabharata its extraordinary character.

It can function as a family saga, a heroic narrative, a collection of legends, a philosophical work and a source of religious and moral instruction.

Its stories can be read independently, but they also contribute to the identity of the larger composition.

The different layers do not necessarily need to have originated together. A narrative tradition can bring together material from different periods, communities and literary settings. A poet or compiler can organise these materials into a larger structure, while later transmission can preserve, expand or rearrange parts of the composition.

The Mahabharata's textual history reflects this complexity.

In the twentieth century, the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute undertook the preparation of a critical edition of the Mahabharata. Scholars compared manuscripts from different regions and manuscript traditions to identify shared readings and reconstruct an earlier recoverable text.

The project took several decades and was completed in 1966.

The Critical Edition does not represent every version of the Mahabharata ever told, nor does it provide a recording of the epic's earliest oral form. Its importance lies in showing the scale of the manuscript tradition and the complexity involved in establishing a critically edited text.

The Mahabharata we encounter today is therefore both a literary composition and the product of a long history of transmission.

An Epic That Contains an Entire World

The Mahabharata is remarkable because its central story is only one part of its enormous narrative structure.

The conflict between the Pandavas and Kauravas provides a central thread, but around it exist generations of family histories, accounts of kings, legends of earlier heroes, philosophical teachings and numerous independent stories.

Some of these narratives explain the main events. Others provide parallels, introduce new characters or explore questions that the central story raises.

The traditional association with Vyasa gives the epic a single recognised identity, but its literary and textual history reveals a much more layered development.

Its stories have also continued beyond the boundaries of the original composition. Krishna's life, the Bhagavad Gita, individual heroic episodes and numerous other narratives have developed their own identities in Indian literature and religious traditions.

The Mahabharata is therefore not simply a story about a war. It is a collection of interconnected narratives that together form an enormous literary world.

And this brings us to an interesting connection between the two great epics.

The Ramayana and the Mahabharata have their own central characters, narrative foundations and traditions. Yet they also contain references to one another, share elements of a wider mythological world and exist within a cultural tradition where stories frequently connect across different compositions.

To understand that relationship, we must look at the two epics together.
