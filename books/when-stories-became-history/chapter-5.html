When Stories Became History

Chapter 5

The Ramayana: The Many Lives of Rama's Story

Few stories have travelled as far, lasted as long or taken as many forms as the story of Rama.

Across India and beyond, generations have grown up listening to the story of a prince who leaves his kingdom, lives in exile, loses his wife to Ravana and eventually returns after a great battle. Its characters have become familiar across languages and cultures. Rama, Sita, Lakshmana, Hanuman and Ravana are recognised far beyond the pages of any single book.

Yet the Ramayana is not limited to one composition or one telling.

There is the Sanskrit Ramayana traditionally attributed to Valmiki. There are versions in Tamil, Bengali, Awadhi, Malayalam and numerous other languages. Buddhist and Jain traditions have their own accounts, while countries across Southeast Asia have adapted the story into their literature, theatre and performing arts.

The central narrative remains recognisable, but the details, characterisations and interpretations can differ considerably.

To understand the many lives of Rama's story, we must begin with the account that became its most influential literary foundation.

Valmiki and the Traditional Account

Tradition identifies the sage Valmiki as the composer of the Ramayana.

According to the traditional account presented within the epic itself, Valmiki first hears the story of Rama from the sage Narada. Later, an encounter with a hunter killing one of a pair of birds inspires him to compose poetry. Brahma then instructs him to compose the story of Rama, including events beyond what ordinary observation could reveal.

Valmiki composes the Ramayana in Sanskrit, and the epic describes how he teaches it to Lava and Kusha, who subsequently recite it before Rama.

This is an important detail. The traditional account does not present the Ramayana merely as a written composition. It also presents it as a narrative meant to be memorised, recited and performed before an audience.

The Valmiki Ramayana is traditionally divided into seven books, known as Kandas: Bala Kanda, Ayodhya Kanda, Aranya Kanda, Kishkindha Kanda, Sundara Kanda, Yuddha Kanda and Uttara Kanda.

Together, they cover Rama's birth and childhood, his exile, the abduction of Sita, his alliance with Sugriva, Hanuman's journey to Lanka, the battle against Ravana and the events following Rama's return.

The traditional attribution to Valmiki gives the epic a central authorial identity. It provides the narrative with a recognised origin and a name through which generations have remembered it.

However, the history of the Ramayana tradition extends beyond the traditional account of its composition.

The Sanskrit text itself has a complex transmission history. Different manuscript traditions preserve variations in wording, episodes and passages. Scholars have studied these differences through comparisons of manuscripts and regional recensions.

The Baroda Critical Edition of the Valmiki Ramayana, prepared by the Oriental Institute, brought together and compared numerous manuscript witnesses in an effort to reconstruct an earlier recoverable form of the text.

This work demonstrates that the Ramayana has a textual history worth examining in its own right. The name Valmiki represents the traditional identity of the composition, while the surviving textual tradition reveals a long history of transmission and preservation.

The story of Rama had a life beyond the manuscript.

The Development of the Ramayana Tradition

Once the Ramayana became an established and widely recognised narrative, it provided material for generations of poets, writers, performers and communities.

Its characters were familiar. Its central events were memorable. Its themes of family, loyalty, duty, power, sacrifice and conflict could be explored in different literary and cultural settings.

This created opportunities for new compositions.

One of the most important developments was the appearance of the Ramayana in regional languages. Poets began presenting Rama's story to audiences through their own linguistic and literary traditions.

Kamban's Ramavataram, composed in Tamil, is among the most celebrated examples. Rather than simply reproducing Valmiki's Sanskrit composition, Kamban presents the narrative through the richness of Tamil poetry and its literary conventions.

In the sixteenth century, Tulsidas composed the Ramcharitmanas in Awadhi. His work became enormously influential in North India, presenting Rama's story through a devotional and philosophical framework that reached audiences far beyond the circles familiar with Sanskrit literature.

Other major works include:

· The Krittivasi Ramayana in Bengali, associated with Krittibas Ojha.

· The Adhyatma Ramayana, a Sanskrit devotional retelling that gives particular attention to Rama's divine nature.

· The Ramayana of Ezhuthachan in Malayalam, commonly known as the Adhyatma Ramayanam Kilippattu.

· The Jagamohana Ramayana in Odia, associated with Balarama Dasa.

These works helped make Rama's story part of the literary and cultural identity of different regions.

They also demonstrate that the Ramayana was not simply translated from one language into another. Each composition brought its own literary style, religious ideas and cultural emphasis.

The same story could be presented as a heroic account, a devotional composition, a philosophical narrative or a work of poetry.

And the more widely the story travelled, the more forms it acquired.

Regional, Buddhist and Jain Ramayanas

Perhaps the most revealing aspect of the Ramayana tradition is that different communities did not always tell the same story in the same way.

Some retellings preserve the familiar sequence of events but alter the treatment of particular characters. Others change important episodes or reinterpret the entire narrative through a different religious worldview.

Buddhist and Jain traditions provide particularly interesting examples.

The Buddhist Ramayana Tradition

One of the best-known Buddhist narratives connected with Rama is the Dasaratha Jataka, found in the Pali Jataka collection.

Here, the central character is Rama-pandita, presented as a previous birth of the Buddha. The narrative shares familiar elements with the Rama tradition, including exile and the relationships between members of the royal family, but its treatment of the characters and events differs from the familiar Valmiki account.

The emphasis is also different. The story is presented within a Buddhist framework, highlighting qualities such as patience, restraint and moral conduct.

The importance of this version is not simply that it tells a different story. It shows how a familiar narrative could be incorporated into another religious tradition and given a new purpose.

The characters remained recognisable, but the meaning of their story changed.

The Jain Ramayana Tradition

Jain literature developed its own extensive Ramayana tradition.

One of its important works is Vimalasuri's Paumacariya, composed in Prakrit. It presents the Rama narrative through Jain religious and philosophical ideas.

The differences are substantial.

In this tradition, Rama is portrayed as a Jain hero who follows a path of non-violence. Lakshmana takes the role of the warrior who ultimately kills Ravana, while Ravana himself receives a more developed background than the familiar portrayal of a straightforward antagonist.

The narrative also follows Jain ideas about karma, rebirth and spiritual liberation. Rama eventually renounces worldly life and moves towards spiritual fulfilment.

This is a significant departure from the familiar account.

The same broad characters and conflict are present, but their roles and ultimate destinies are interpreted through a different worldview.

Why These Versions Matter

These Buddhist and Jain accounts reveal something important about the life of a popular narrative.

A story can enter a new religious tradition without simply being copied word for word. Its characters can be reinterpreted, its events rearranged and its moral emphasis changed.

The narrative remains connected to Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Ravana, but it becomes something that reflects the ideas of the community telling it.

This is one reason the Ramayana cannot be understood only through a single text. Its wider history includes many communities that adopted the story and made it their own.

The Ramayana Beyond India

The story of Rama did not remain confined to the Indian subcontinent.

It travelled into Southeast Asia, where it became part of the literature, visual arts, theatre and cultural traditions of several societies.

Thailand developed the Ramakien, its own major adaptation of the Ramayana. The story became closely associated with Thai royal and cultural traditions, and its characters appear in murals, dance performances and other artistic forms.

Cambodia developed the Reamker, a Khmer adaptation that became an important part of the country's literary and performing heritage. Episodes from the narrative appear in temple art and traditional dance.

Java developed the Kakawin Ramayana, a literary composition in Old Javanese. The story also became deeply embedded in Indonesian performance traditions, particularly the wayang shadow-puppet theatre.

These adaptations demonstrate how a narrative can cross linguistic and geographical boundaries and become part of a different cultural environment.

The characters may retain familiar identities, but the way they are presented can change. Local artistic traditions, religious ideas, performance styles and cultural values influence how the story is understood.

The Ramayana therefore became more than an Indian literary tradition. It developed into a shared cultural narrative across a much wider region.

Its journey also shows that stories do not need to remain identical to remain recognisable. A narrative can travel, adapt and acquire new forms while retaining its connection to an older tradition.

Characters, Episodes and Interpretations Across Versions

The most interesting differences between Ramayana traditions are often found not in the central plot but in the way individual characters and episodes are presented.

Consider Rama himself.

In the Valmiki tradition, Rama is presented as an extraordinary prince and an ideal figure, while the epic also explores his human emotions, decisions and responsibilities. In devotional traditions such as Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas, Rama's divine nature receives much greater emphasis.

The character remains Rama, but the reader's relationship with him changes.

Ravana provides another example. In the familiar account, he is the powerful ruler of Lanka whose abduction of Sita leads to the great conflict. In Jain retellings, his character is presented through a different moral and philosophical framework, with a more developed role within Jain universal history.

Hanuman's character also takes on different forms. Across many Indian traditions, he is celebrated for devotion, courage and extraordinary strength. In Southeast Asian adaptations, his adventures and personality can receive different emphasis, sometimes making him a more prominent and playful figure.

Even Sita's story has been interpreted in different ways.

Her experiences, decisions, suffering and relationship with Rama have been explored through different literary and devotional traditions. Some versions give greater attention to her inner strength and independence, while others emphasise her devotion and endurance.

Individual episodes can also change in their presentation and significance. The treatment of Ahalya, Shabari, the golden deer, the encounter with Hanuman and the events following the return from Lanka can differ across retellings.

These differences matter because they show that a familiar narrative can generate many interpretations without losing its central identity.

A reader encountering one version may understand a character in a particular way. Another version may present the same character through a different set of motivations and values.

Neither the characters nor the story exist only in one literary expression.

One Story, Many Lives

The Ramayana offers a remarkable example of how a narrative can grow beyond its earliest recognised composition.

It has been recited, performed, translated, rewritten and reinterpreted across generations. It has entered different languages, religious traditions and cultural environments. Its characters have appeared in poetry, theatre, paintings, sculpture, dance and public celebrations.

Valmiki's name remains central to the traditional identity of the epic, but the larger Ramayana tradition includes the contributions of countless poets, performers, translators and communities.

Some preserved familiar episodes. Others introduced new interpretations. Some gave greater attention to particular characters, while others reshaped the narrative around their own religious and cultural ideas.

The result is not a single unchanging account but a vast family of connected narratives.

And this raises an interesting question.

If the story of Rama could develop so many forms across different regions and traditions, what about the other great epic? The Mahabharata, with its enormous collection of characters, genealogies, legends and stories within stories, offers an even more expansive example.

To understand that narrative, we must now turn to Vyasa and the world of the Mahabharata.
