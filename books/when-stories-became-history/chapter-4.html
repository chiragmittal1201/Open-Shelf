When Stories Became History

Chapter 4

When Stories Start Growing

A story that captures people's attention rarely remains limited to its original form.

Once people become familiar with its characters and events, they begin wanting more. They want to know what happened before the main story, what happened afterwards, and what other characters were doing while the central events unfolded.

A popular hero may inspire stories about childhood, family and early adventures. A supporting character may become interesting enough to deserve a story of their own. A passing reference to an earlier conflict may become the starting point for an entirely new narrative.

And sometimes, two stories that once existed independently begin to connect.

This is how a narrative can gradually develop into something much larger than its original account. It is no longer just one story. It becomes a collection of connected stories, characters and events that together create an expansive narrative world.

We see this happening in modern entertainment all the time. A successful film gets a sequel. A popular character receives a spin-off. A series explores events that happened before the original story, while another introduces characters who eventually become important elsewhere.

Ancient storytelling had no modern production studios or franchise plans, but the human interest behind this process was much the same: people wanted to hear more about stories and characters they already loved.

New Episodes, Characters and Connections

One of the simplest ways to expand a story is to explore something it has only briefly mentioned.

Suppose a narrative describes a great warrior who appears during an important battle. The original account may focus entirely on his actions during that conflict. But audiences may become curious about his life before the battle.

Who trained him? Where did he come from? What made him such a formidable warrior? Did he have rivals, friends or family?

These questions create opportunities for new stories.

An existing character can receive a more detailed background. An earlier generation can become the subject of another narrative. A minor incident can be expanded into an important episode.

The same process can introduce entirely new characters. A storyteller may develop a companion, rival, relative or mentor whose role gradually becomes more significant. These characters can then develop their own relationships and experiences.

Over time, the original narrative begins accumulating stories around itself.

Indian epic traditions offer several interesting examples.

The Mahabharata contains numerous narratives within its central account. The story of Nala and Damayanti, the tale of Savitri and Satyavan, and the story of Rishyasringa are among the many episodes that appear within the larger epic. These narratives have their own characters, conflicts and conclusions, yet they exist within the broader Mahabharata tradition.

The Harivamsha provides another example of how a narrative world can expand. Traditionally associated with the Mahabharata, it contains material concerning the genealogies of the Yadavas and the life and exploits of Krishna, extending the world of characters and events associated with the larger epic tradition.

The Ramayana tradition also developed beyond its central narrative. Stories about Rama's ancestors, the lives of other characters and events connected to the main journey became subjects of separate compositions and regional retellings.

Not every additional episode needs to originate from the same storyteller or period. Different narratives can be connected over time, creating a much larger collection of related stories.

A character who once occupied only a small part of a narrative can eventually become the centre of another.

Oral Transmission and Human Memory

Before written texts became widely available, storytelling depended heavily on human memory.

People remembered narratives, recited them and passed them to others. A story could travel across generations through repeated performances, sometimes remaining within a particular community and sometimes moving between different regions.

But human memory is not a recording device.

People remember the overall meaning of an experience, the important events, the emotions and the details that stand out. When they recall something, they reconstruct it using what they already know and understand.

Psychologist Frederic Bartlett explored this process in his influential book Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology (1932). His research demonstrated how memory is influenced by existing knowledge and cultural expectations. When people recall unfamiliar material, they may reorganise details to make the account more understandable and consistent with what they already know.

This has an interesting connection with storytelling.

A narrator may remember the central events of a story while presenting certain details differently. Another storyteller may emphasise a different character or explain an event in a new way. Over generations, these differences can contribute to the development of distinct versions.

However, oral transmission should not be confused with careless repetition.

India provides a remarkable example of how oral traditions can preserve material with extraordinary precision. Vedic recitation developed highly sophisticated methods of memorisation and pronunciation. Different recitation patterns helped preserve the wording and sequence of sacred texts with exceptional care.

This demonstrates that oral transmission can be highly disciplined and accurate.

At the same time, not every storytelling tradition operates under the same rules. A carefully memorised text and a narrative performed for a changing audience serve different purposes.

A Vedic reciter may be expected to preserve an established sequence of words. A storyteller performing a heroic narrative may have greater freedom in its presentation, emphasis and interpretation.

Both are forms of oral transmission, but they do not necessarily produce the same kind of continuity.

This distinction is important. Stories can be preserved accurately in one tradition while developing different forms in another.

Additions, Omissions and Interpretations

When a story travels through different storytellers and communities, three things can influence its development: what gets added, what gets left out and how existing events are interpreted.

An addition may introduce a new character, a conversation, an explanation or an entire episode. An omission may remove a detail that a particular storyteller considers less important. An interpretation may give a familiar event a different meaning without changing the event itself.

Consider a simple example.

A story describes a king who refuses a request. The original account does not explain his reasons. A later storyteller may suggest that he was protecting his kingdom. Another may present the refusal as an act of pride. A third may introduce an earlier incident to explain why the king behaved that way.

The central event remains the same, but its meaning changes.

This is one of the ways narratives develop depth. Characters acquire motivations, decisions receive explanations and events become connected through additional details.

The same process can also affect the structure of a story. An episode that was once brief may become a major turning point. A character who was previously treated as a villain may receive a more sympathetic background. An event may be presented from the perspective of someone who was barely mentioned in an earlier version.

The Ramayana tradition provides a particularly clear example of how interpretations can differ.

Valmiki's Ramayana, the Tamil Ramavataram of Kamban, the Ramcharitmanas of Tulsidas, and the Jain Paumacariya associated with Vimalasuri all present versions of Rama's story. They share recognisable elements, but their treatment of characters, episodes and religious ideas differs.

In Many Ramayanas: The Diversity of a Narrative Tradition in South Asia (1991), edited by Paula Richman, scholars examine how different communities and literary traditions have interpreted the Ramayana.

These differences are not limited to changes in language. They can involve the presentation of characters, the meaning of events and the values emphasised by a particular version.

A story can therefore remain recognisably the same while developing a different identity in another cultural setting.

Regional Variations and Shared Narrative Worlds

India's storytelling traditions developed across a vast geographical and linguistic landscape.

Different communities had their own languages, customs, religious traditions and artistic practices. When a popular narrative reached a new region, it could become part of that region's cultural life.

This produced an extraordinary variety of retellings.

The Ramayana is perhaps the clearest example. Beyond Valmiki's Sanskrit composition, there are major versions in Tamil, Bengali, Assamese, Telugu, Malayalam and numerous other languages.

The Buddhist Dasaratha Jataka presents a version of the Rama narrative within a Buddhist framework. Jain traditions developed their own accounts, including Vimalasuri's Paumacariya. These works differ in important ways from the familiar Sanskrit narrative.

The story also travelled beyond the Indian subcontinent.

Thailand developed the Ramakien, Cambodia the Reamker, and Java produced the Kakawin Ramayana. These works adapted the narrative to their own literary, artistic and cultural environments.

The Mahabharata tradition also travelled through different languages and performance traditions, developing regional forms and interpretations.

What makes these examples especially interesting is that the stories did not simply move from one place to another unchanged. They became part of the societies that adopted them.

A familiar character could be presented differently. An episode could receive greater emphasis. Religious and philosophical ideas could influence how the narrative was understood.

This is how a shared narrative world can develop without every community telling precisely the same story.

The characters and central events provide a point of connection, while regional traditions give them new expressions.

The Influence of Audiences and Storytellers

A storyteller does not work in isolation.

Every performance takes place before an audience, and every audience brings its own interests, expectations and understanding to the story.

Some characters attract greater attention than others. Certain episodes become particularly popular. Listeners may want to hear more about a specific event or understand the reasons behind a character's actions.

A storyteller who recognises this interest has an opportunity to explore those parts of the narrative further.

A dramatic battle may receive a longer description. A popular character may receive additional episodes. A relationship that was only briefly mentioned may become an important part of the story.

The audience's response can influence what gets remembered and repeated.

This does not mean that every audience directly rewrote the stories or that every storyteller changed the narrative to satisfy listeners. The process is more gradual. Stories that hold attention are more likely to be remembered, repeated and carried forward. Material that becomes meaningful to a community can gain a lasting place within its tradition.

Researchers of oral traditions have studied this relationship between performers, audiences and the development of narratives.

In The Singer of Tales (1960), Albert Lord examined how oral epic singers compose and perform narratives through traditional techniques, formulaic expressions and familiar story patterns. His research showed that oral performance is not necessarily the reproduction of a fixed script. It can involve creative composition within an established tradition.

This gives us a useful way to understand how a narrative can remain familiar while its individual performances differ.

A storyteller can follow a recognised sequence of events while presenting it with a distinctive style. Another performer may emphasise different episodes or use different expressions. Over time, such performances can contribute to the development of the tradition itself.

The audience and storyteller are therefore both part of the life of a narrative. One brings the story to the gathering; the other gives it a reason to continue being told.

When Separate Stories Become One

Perhaps the most interesting stage in the growth of a narrative occurs when separate stories begin connecting with one another.

A popular hero may have a story about his childhood, another about his family, another about his greatest battle and yet another about what happened to his descendants.

Initially, these accounts may circulate independently. But as storytellers connect their characters and events, they begin forming a larger narrative structure.

This is particularly visible in the Mahabharata, where stories of different generations, kingdoms, families and individuals come together within one enormous composition.

The epic does not simply describe the conflict between the Pandavas and Kauravas. It contains genealogies, accounts of earlier generations, philosophical discussions, legends and numerous independent narratives.

The Ramayana tradition also connects stories of different generations and characters to the central account of Rama.

What begins as a collection of individual narratives can gradually become an interconnected world.

And once that happens, the possibilities for storytelling become enormous. A character can have a history before the central events and a legacy afterwards. A separate legend can become connected to a familiar epic. One narrative can provide the background for another.

The story no longer belongs to a single moment or a single storyteller. It becomes a tradition that can continue developing.

This brings us to the two great epics themselves.

The Ramayana and the Mahabharata are not merely long stories. They contain entire worlds of characters, relationships, conflicts, traditions and ideas. To understand how these narrative worlds came to be, we must now look at each epic more closely, beginning with the many lives of Rama's story.
