When Stories Became History

Chapter 3

From Events to Extraordinary Narratives

Every great story has a beginning, but it does not necessarily begin as a great story.

Sometimes it begins with a person whose actions attract attention. Sometimes it begins with a conflict between two families, a battle between rival kingdoms, a journey across unfamiliar territory or an event that a community continues to remember long after it has passed.

At first, the account may be relatively simple. Someone remembers what happened and tells another person. The account reaches a new audience, becomes part of local memory and eventually finds its way into storytelling.

But stories rarely remain limited to the bare sequence of events. People become interested in the individuals involved, their motivations, their relationships and the circumstances surrounding them. Details are added, characters become more distinctive, and events are connected to create a larger narrative.

An incident can become a legend. A remembered leader can become a celebrated hero. A conflict between people can become a struggle involving extraordinary forces.

This is one way to understand how ordinary accounts can develop into extraordinary narratives.

Historical Memories and Local Legends

History does not always begin with written records. Communities preserve memories through genealogies, songs, oral accounts, local traditions and stories about important people and events.

A ruler remembered for military victories may become the subject of heroic accounts. A major conflict may survive in the collective memory of a region. A journey, migration or political struggle may become associated with particular families, places or communities.

As these memories are passed down, they become part of a community's understanding of its own past.

Ancient India had traditions of preserving genealogies and accounts of rulers and lineages. The Sanskrit term ākhyāna refers to a narrative or account, and such traditions formed part of the wider storytelling culture in which heroic and historical material circulated.

Historian Romila Thapar, particularly in The Past Before Us: Historical Traditions of Early North India (2013), examines how early Indian societies remembered and represented their past. She discusses the importance of genealogies, narratives, dynastic traditions and accounts of heroes in the development of historical consciousness.

These traditions are particularly interesting because they existed in a space where memory, social identity and storytelling could overlap.

A community might remember an important ancestor, but the account of that ancestor could also become a source of pride. His achievements might be repeated at gatherings, his lineage might be celebrated, and his story might become associated with the identity of the community itself.

Over time, the importance of the person could grow alongside the story.

The same process can happen with places. A location associated with an important event may become part of a larger narrative. A particular forest, river, kingdom or battlefield can acquire significance because of the stories connected to it.

This gives narratives a foundation that audiences can recognise: people, places, relationships and events drawn from the world around them.

But a foundation is not the same as the finished structure. Once a story begins travelling, it can develop in many directions.

Heroes, Conflicts and Legendary Characters

People have always been fascinated by extraordinary individuals.

A person who displays courage, intelligence, strength, leadership or unusual determination naturally attracts attention. Their actions become memorable, and their reputation can spread beyond the immediate community.

This is how heroes emerge in storytelling.

A successful warrior may become famous for a battle. A ruler may be remembered for establishing a kingdom. A traveller may become known for an extraordinary journey. A person who stands against overwhelming opposition may become the central character of a popular tale.

But a hero is not interesting merely because of what they accomplish. Audiences also want to know who they are.

Where did they come from? What made them different? Who were their parents? Who were their friends and rivals? What challenges did they face before becoming famous?

These questions provide opportunities to expand a character's story.

A narrative about a warrior's victory can grow into an account of his childhood, family, training, rivalries and personal struggles. Characters who were once mentioned briefly can become important in their own right. A rival can receive a history, a motivation and a following of his own.

The conflict itself can also become larger.

A disagreement between two individuals may develop into a conflict between families. A family dispute may become a struggle between kingdoms. The actions of a few people may eventually be presented as a battle with consequences for an entire society.

This is how a relatively focused account can develop into an elaborate narrative world.

The characters no longer exist only to explain one event. They become individuals whose lives, relationships and decisions provide material for many stories.

Imagination, Exaggeration and Dramatic Expansion

A story becomes memorable when it gives its audience something worth remembering.

A battle can become more dramatic through descriptions of bravery and sacrifice. A journey can become extraordinary through dangerous encounters. A character's strength can be emphasised until it becomes almost superhuman.

This is where imagination enters the process of storytelling.

Consider how a historical event is presented in modern cinema. The film Dangal, for example, is based on the real achievements of wrestlers Geeta Phogat and Babita Phogat. Their sporting journey provides the foundation, but the film also uses dramatic scenes, conversations, conflicts and character interactions to create a complete cinematic experience.

The audience is not simply watching a record of sporting results. It is experiencing a story.

The same distinction helps us understand how narratives can develop over time. An event may provide the starting point, while the storytelling surrounding it gives the event greater emotional depth, dramatic structure and memorable characters.

A character's courage may become legendary. An ordinary journey may acquire extraordinary obstacles. A victory may be described in increasingly grand terms. An unexplained event may receive an imaginative explanation.

And once extraordinary elements become part of a popular narrative, they can become some of its most memorable features.

Indian epics provide striking examples of this kind of storytelling. The Ramayana includes extraordinary journeys, divine weapons, powerful beings and events on a vast scale. The Mahabharata presents an enormous conflict involving heroic warriors, complex family relationships, divine intervention and philosophical questions.

These elements make the narratives much more than straightforward accounts of political or military events. They create worlds that audiences can explore through characters, adventures and moral conflicts.

Importantly, dramatic expansion does not have to mean that someone deliberately set out to change an account. Storytelling naturally rewards memorable details. A performer wants to hold an audience's attention, a poet wants to create a compelling composition, and listeners want narratives that move and engage them.

The more powerful the story becomes, the more likely it is to be remembered and retold.

The Possibility of Multiple Contributors

We often associate a great literary work with a single author. A name becomes attached to the composition, and that name comes to represent the entire work.

In Indian tradition, the Ramayana is associated with Valmiki, while the Mahabharata is associated with Vyasa. These figures occupy central positions in the traditional accounts of the epics.

Yet the development of a large narrative tradition and the creation of a literary composition are not necessarily the same process.

A poet may organise existing material into a coherent composition. Earlier narratives may provide characters, genealogies or episodes. Later transmitters, performers and authors may preserve, interpret, expand or reorganise parts of the tradition.

This is particularly relevant when we consider the scale of the two great epics.

The Mahabharata contains an enormous range of material: genealogies, heroic narratives, philosophical discussions, teachings, legends and stories within stories. The Ramayana also exists in a wide range of regional and literary traditions, each presenting the central narrative through its own cultural and artistic perspective.

In The Sanskrit Epics (1998), John Brockington examines the literary development, composition and textual history of the Ramayana and Mahabharata. His work demonstrates the complexity of treating these epics as simple, unchanging compositions.

The Mahabharata, in particular, is a layered work whose development involved a long process of composition and transmission. Its traditional association with Vyasa remains central to its identity, while its literary history reveals a much more complex development.

This distinction opens up an interesting way of looking at authorship.

A single name can represent the identity of a great composition without necessarily accounting for every stage through which its narrative traditions developed.

Think of a story that begins with one central character. Over time, other storytellers introduce related characters, explore their backgrounds and connect their experiences with the original narrative. A poet eventually brings these materials together into a larger composition.

The result may be remembered as one great work, even though the material behind it has a much longer history.

This is not an unfamiliar process in literature. Stories, legends and characters have often developed through the contributions of different writers and performers. The original idea may remain recognisable even as the larger narrative continues to grow.

From a Memory to a World of Stories

We can now see how several different elements come together.

A community remembers an event. A person becomes known for extraordinary actions. A storyteller turns the account into an engaging narrative. Other performers carry it to new audiences. Characters receive greater attention, conflicts become more elaborate, and additional episodes begin to connect with the original story.

What started as an account of something becomes a narrative that people return to for entertainment, emotion, identity and meaning.

And there is another important development.

Once a story becomes popular, people do not necessarily want to hear only the original events. They become interested in everything surrounding them. They want to know what happened before the main story. What happened afterwards, what other characters were doing and how different events connect.

A hero's childhood can become a story of its own. A supporting character can receive an independent narrative. An old conflict can be connected to a new one. Separate legends can become part of a shared world.

The story has grown beyond its beginning.

This brings us to the next stage: how these narratives continued to expand, how new episodes and characters entered them, and how different versions could develop across generations.

Because once a story becomes popular, its journey is often only beginning.
